# RFC 12: Round Replay: Position Tracking and a 3D Viewer

> **Status: built and measured once on the real server (2026-10-19, 8 bots).** The backend, the bridge and the viewer all work. Real rounds with real players have been replayed and looked at since (the maintainer, several times). The death heat map (3.6) is built as the killzones of [RFC 21](./RFC-021.md). The 16-bot, 300-second round is measured (section 13). Other heat maps are not part of this RFC. It builds on [RFC 10](./RFC-010.md) (the map's floor plans and their levels) and on [RFC 11](./RFC-011.md) (the map's exact 3D geometry as a glTF file, which the viewer uses **when it exists**) and on the existing round stats (`stats.lua`, migration v7, `POST /api/v1/round`), which have no RFC of their own and are summarised in section 3.1.

> The sampling settings can be changed from the dashboard ([RFC 23](./RFC-023.md)).

## 1. Abstract
The dashboard knows who played, which role they had, who won and who killed whom, but not **where anything happened**. This RFC adds an opt-in, per-round **position track**: while a round runs, the game server records where each living player is, **as often as it can without hurting play** (an adaptive rate under a measured time budget, section 3.2), and sends the whole track once when the round is over. The dashboard stores it next to the round and shows a **replay for signed-in users** ([RFC 13](./RFC-013.md): any Steam account; a replay is not public) drawn **on the map's 3D geometry when there is one** (RFC 11), or on its floor plan (RFC 10) when there is not: a marker per player, a timeline, and the deaths marked, with a free 3D camera, a top-down view and a follow-the-player view. Nothing is recorded unless the server owner turns it on, nothing is sent while the round runs, and the recording can never affect the game: it follows the same hard rule as the round stats.

## 2. Motivation
*   **"What happened in that round?"** is the question a TTT admin asks most: a death that looks like RDM (rule-breaking killing), a traitor who was never caught, a round that ended in thirty seconds. Today the dashboard shows a result and a list of deaths with a time. A replay shows who was standing where, who walked up to whom, and who was alone with a body.
*   **The groundwork exists.** RFC 10 produces a floor plan per map, split into levels, with no manual work. A replay needs exactly that: a background in the same coordinates as the positions, and a way to tell which level a position is on. When the map's exact geometry is available (RFC 11), the replay can be shown **in the map itself**: the rooms, stairs and walls the players really moved through, which is far easier to follow than dots on a flat plan.
*   **Aggregates come for free.** Once positions are stored, a heat map (where do deaths happen on this map, where do traitors wait) is a query, not another feature on the game server.

## 3. Design

### 3.1 What exists (summary, not a change)
`stats.lua` follows one round at a time: `TTTBeginRound` creates a round with an ID and the players who are alive, `PlayerDeath` appends a death, `TTTEndRound` closes it and posts it once to `/api/v1/round`. Its rules, which this RFC keeps: hooks only copy small values into memory; every hook body runs inside `pcall`; the one HTTP request is asynchronous and happens after the round has ended; a failed request is logged and dropped, with no queue and no retry; buffers are capped; a ConVar switches everything off without a restart. The backend stores `rounds`, `round_players` and `deaths` and answers a repeated `round_id` with success and no second row.

### 3.2 Recording (bridge)
*   **Opt-in:** a new ConVar `traitordash_track` (default `0`) switches the recording on. It is separate from `traitordash_stats` so that switching on the stats does not start collecting positions.
*   **Sampling, as fast as it can safely go:** a single repeating timer (not `Think`) samples every living player's `GetPos()` and view yaw at one **shared tick**, and the *interval between ticks is chosen by the bridge itself*, between a fastest and a slowest allowed value (`traitordash_track_interval_min`, default 0.2 s, i.e. 5 samples a second, and `traitordash_track_interval_max`, default 1 s; the fastest can be set as low as 0.05 s, twenty samples a second, since 2026-11-05). The goal is the highest rate that does not hurt play, and "does not hurt" is **measured, not assumed**:
    *   **A time budget.** Every sampling tick times its own body (`SysTime`). The budget is `traitordash_track_budget_ms` (default 0.5 ms per tick). If the average cost of the last ten ticks is above it, the interval grows by half (up to the slowest value); when the cost is below a third of it and the server is healthy, the interval shrinks by a quarter (down to the fastest value).
    *   **Server health.** The server's own frame time is compared with its tick interval. If a frame took more than 1.5 times the tick interval, the server is busy and the interval grows; if it took more than 3 times, sampling **skips that tick** instead of adding work to a server that is already struggling. A skipped tick is recorded as a gap, never invented.
    *   **Degraded, not harmful.** If even the slowest interval cannot meet the budget, sampling pauses and the track says so (`degraded`), so a replay shows a gap and an admin can see why.
    *   **What dominates in practice** is data volume, not CPU: reading two getters for 32 players is microseconds. So the fastest default is set by size (3.3), and the budget is the safety net for a slow server, a big player count or a heavy addon.
    *   **Reported.** At the end of every round the bridge prints one line: the number of ticks, the interval range actually used, the average and worst tick cost, skipped ticks, and the size of the track. That line is also how the setting is tuned on a real server (section 7).
*   **Cost and safety:** a tick is a loop over at most 128 players that calls two getters; the work is bounded by the existing player cap, and the timer body is inside `pcall` with the same kill switch as the stats.
*   **Encoding (in memory, as it is recorded):** one global list `t` of tick times (whole milliseconds since the round began, stored as differences, because the interval varies); per player, `x`, `y`, `z` as whole numbers divided by 8 (rounded) and `yaw` as 0-255 (`360 / 256` degrees per step), stored as the first absolute value and then **differences** to that player's previous sample. At 5 samples a second a difference is tiny; a teleport is a large one and stays correct.
*   **When a player appears and disappears:** a player's track starts at the first tick they are alive in the round and ends at their death (a final sample is taken in the death hook, so the death position is exact) or at the end of the round. A revived player (necromancer) continues in a second segment. Spectators are never tracked.
*   **Deaths** get a position: the victim's, and the attacker's when there is one, taken in `PlayerDeath`, plus the weapon class when TTT2 supplies it. This is a small addition to what `stats.lua` records today.
*   **Caps:** at most 128 players, 40 000 ticks per round (raised from 20 000 on 2026-11-05 with the faster floor: 33 minutes at the fastest rate of 0.05 s, 66 at 0.1 s, 11 hours at the slowest), 8 MB for the whole encoded track. A round that would exceed a cap stops recording and the track is sent truncated, with a flag saying so. When the track is approaching its size cap, the interval is raised instead of the round being cut short.
*   **Memory:** the buffers are plain Lua numbers in per-player arrays; a 15-minute round with 32 players at 5 samples a second is about 580 000 numbers, a few tens of MB of Lua memory at worst, released when the round is sent; the per-round caps bound it.

*   **As built (step 2):** `addon/lua/traitordash/track.lua`, called from `stats.lua` (begin, death, end) so the track and the round share their ID and their players, and the track goes out in the round's success callback. Decisions the RFC left open: sampling starts at the **fastest** interval and backs off; the average cost is judged over **ten** ticks and the ring is cleared after each change so the new rate is measured fresh; a **degraded** round stops sampling for good (it does not resume), keeps what it has and is flagged; a **skipped** tick adds no entry to `t` and ends every open segment, so the gap shows as the break between segments; **a death takes an extra tick** in which *every* living player is sampled (so all tracks stay on one shared time axis) and the victim's segment ends there; deaths are recorded even when sampling has stopped; the size is estimated (16 bytes a sample), the interval is forced to its slowest above 60% of 7 MB and recording stops at 7 MB, a margin below the backend's 8 MB; the real encoded size is checked before sending. A step longer than 60 s is clamped (the backend refuses more) and a player has at most 64 segments. A switch-off (`traitordash_track 0`) mid-round drops the recording and sends nothing. A `404` from the track endpoint stops the attempts until the map changes. **To measure on a real server (step 4):** the cost of `util.TableToJSON` on a large track at round end (a hitch, not a per-tick cost) and the size per minute.

### 3.3 Upload (bridge to backend)
*   After the round has been accepted (the success callback of `/api/v1/round`), the bridge sends the track once to `POST /api/v1/track`, authenticated with the server's token like every bridge request. If the round was rejected, or the request fails, the track is dropped, exactly as a lost round is: no queue, no retry.
*   **Body:** `round_id`, `map`, the map file's fingerprint (RFC 10, 3.1; absent if unknown), `truncated`, `degraded`, the tick times `t`, the players (their IDs, as in the round), their position segments, and the deaths with positions. All numbers are integers.

```jsonc
{
  "round_id": "a1b2c3d4-0001", "map": "ttt_example", "fingerprint": "ab12...",
  "truncated": false, "degraded": false,
  "t": [0, 200, 200, 400, 200, ...],     // tick times: milliseconds since the round began, as differences
  "players": [
    { "id": "7656119...", "segments": [
        { "from": 0,                       // index into t of this segment's first sample
          "x": [120, 3, -1, ...],          // first value absolute (units / 8), then differences
          "y": [-80, 0, 4, ...], "z": [30, 0, 0, ...], "a": [12, 2, -1, ...] } ] } ],
  "deaths": [ { "victim": "7656119...", "attacker": "7656119...", "t": 94,
                "vx": 410, "vy": -20, "vz": 36, "ax": 400, "ay": -22, "az": 36, "weapon": "weapon_ttt_m16" } ]
}
```
*   **Size:** each sample is four small numbers, about 16 bytes of JSON. At the fastest default (5 a second) 20 players over a 5-minute round is 30 000 samples, about 0.5 MB; a 15-minute round with 32 players is about 2.3 MB. At 10 a second those double (differences are smaller, so slightly less). The body is limited to **8 MB**. Compressing the body (GMod's `util.Compress` is LZMA, which the backend would have to decode) would cut it to a third and is listed as an open question rather than assumed.

### 3.4 Storage (backend)
*   **As built (step 1):** the limits card with the usage line is on the server's stats page, for admins (`POST /servers/<id>/tracks/limits`); pinning is `POST /servers/<id>/rounds/<round_id>/pin` (`pin=1` or `pin=0`, `409` at the pin maximum). Both need an admin session, are refused from another site and are audited (`TRACK_LIMITS_CHANGED`, `TRACK_PINNED`, `TRACK_UNPINNED`).  `POST /api/v1/track` (`services/tracks.ts` checks, `Store` stores), migration **v13**, retention and pinning in `Store`, the instance settings read at start-up (an invalid one stops the start with a message). Death times `t` are **milliseconds since the round began**, death positions are in the same units as the track (whole numbers of 8 units). The body limit is **8 MB** everywhere (an earlier draft said 2 MB in 4.3 and 7). A position's first value must be within +-4096 (+-32768 units) and stay there when the differences are added; the view angle stays within 0-255; a tick is at most a minute after the last; a round is at most six hours; a player has at most 64 segments.
*   **Endpoint:** `POST /api/v1/track` validates strictly (round exists for this server and has no track yet; player IDs belong to that round; array lengths agree; every number is an integer within the map's plausible range; segments do not overlap in time; counts within the caps) and answers `400` for anything else, and `413` over 8 MB. A repeated upload for the same round is answered with success and changes nothing. Rate limited per server, like the round endpoint.
*   **Table, migration v13** (RFC 11 took v11 and v12; the numbers follow the order in which they were built): `round_tracks(server_id, round_id, map, fingerprint, truncated, degraded, bytes, created_at, pinned, pinned_by, pinned_at, data)`, one row per round, `data` the validated track as JSON text. The key is `(server_id, round_id)`; the row is deleted with the round and with the server.
*   **Retention:** tracks are far larger than rounds, so they have their own limits: by default the **newest 200 tracks and 200 MB per server**, oldest deleted first. Both numbers are configurable, on three levels, so a small instance can keep fewer and a server under investigation can keep more:
    1.  **Built-in defaults:** 200 tracks and 200 MB per server.
    2.  **Instance level (the operator):** the environment variables `TRACKS_KEEP_COUNT` and `TRACKS_KEEP_MB` change the defaults for every server, and `TRACKS_MAX_COUNT` and `TRACKS_MAX_MB` set a **ceiling** that no per-server setting can exceed (defaults 2000 tracks and 2000 MB). The ceiling is what protects the backend's disk, which is shared by every server on the instance; a value that is not a whole number, or a default above its own ceiling, is refused at start-up with a message.
    3.  **Per server (an admin, in the dashboard):** the server's page has two fields, "Keep at most N tracks" and "... at most M MB", that override the instance defaults for that server, within the ceiling and at least 1; an empty field means "use the default". `0` is allowed for both and means **record nothing**: tracks are accepted and discarded, which is how to stop storing without touching the game server. The change is recorded in the audit log with the admin's SteamID.
    *   **When a limit applies:** after every stored track and at start-up (so lowering a limit takes effect at the next upload at the latest, and immediately after a restart). The count and the size are checked together; whichever is exceeded first removes the oldest tracks until both hold. A track bigger than the whole size limit is refused with a `413` that says which limit it hit. A round keeps its stats after its track has been removed.
    *   **Pinning:** an admin can **pin** a round (a button on the replay page, same-site `Origin` required, audited with the admin's SteamID, and undone the same way). A pinned track is never removed by the count or size limits, which apply to the unpinned tracks. So that pins cannot defeat the disk limit, the instance has `TRACKS_MAX_PINNED` (default 50 per server) and pinned tracks count towards the shown usage; at the maximum, pinning is refused with a message saying to unpin one first. Deleting a pinned track's round or its server still removes it.
*   **Visibility of use:** the same dashboard section shows what is used: "37 tracks, 41 MB of 200 tracks / 200 MB", so an admin can see how much room is left before changing anything, and how many tracks are pinned.

### 3.5 The viewer (dashboard)
*   **Where:** `GET /servers/<id>/rounds/<round_id>/replay`, linked from the round list in the stats pages for rounds that have a track. **It needs a signed-in user** (RFC 13): an anonymous visitor who follows the link is sent to Steam sign-in and brought back to the replay; the round list shows the link to everyone, marked as needing sign-in. Data comes from `GET /servers/<id>/rounds/<round_id>/track.json`, gated the same way. Bot-only test rounds are hidden unless `?bots=1`, as in the stats.
*   **One viewer, three backgrounds.** The viewer is a single three.js scene (RFC 11, 3.6: MIT, loaded only on this page, about 160 KB gzipped, cached afterwards) that draws the best background available for the map and puts the players into the same coordinates:
    1.  **The map's 3D geometry (preferred).** If the map has exact geometry (RFC 11), the viewer loads its GLB: one node per level, floors and walls. The players are drawn inside it.
    2.  **The floor plan as tiles.** Without a mesh but with a trace plan (RFC 10), the viewer builds a flat tile for every cell with a floor, at its height, one group per level, coloured as the plan pictures are. The same scene, camera and controls work; the map is just thinner.
    3.  **Nothing.** With neither, positions are drawn on an empty grid fitted to the track's bounds, and the page says there is no map for this round.
    The page says which background it uses and, for a plan or a mesh made for another version of the map (fingerprints differ), that it may not match.
*   **Cameras.** *Orbit* (drag to rotate, wheel or pinch to zoom, the 3D view), *top-down* (an orthographic camera straight above, which is the 2D replay as a special case, not a second renderer) and *follow* (the camera trails the selected player, looking where they look). Phones: one-finger orbit, two-finger zoom and pan.
*   **Levels.** The levels are the storeys of RFC 10; in a 3D map a **level selector** hides the levels above the chosen one (or cuts them away with a clipping plane), so the player on the ground floor is not hidden by the roof; by default the viewer **follows the selected player's level**. A position's level is the storey whose height is nearest, so the backend sends the storey heights with the track (from the mesh's level nodes, or from the plan).
*   **Colouring (2026-11-06):** the map is always drawn textured, in the replay as on the map page. The Textured / Height / Levels switch was removed from both (nobody used the other two); a replay used to take whatever was last chosen on the map page, with no way to change it.
*   **Players (as built, revised after the first look at real data):** a player is a low-poly person **at the true size of a Source player** (a box 32 units wide and 72 tall, the eyes at 64, so doors, stairs and rooms around them have the right proportions), in the **team's colour**, with a nose for where they face (the white arrow on the ground of the first version was removed, RFC 22, 4d), and a short fading trail. **Nothing is ever enlarged to be seen** (the first version scaled the markers with the map, up to about four times, and the selected player by another third, so nothing was to scale and sizes varied). What keeps a player findable from far away is a **pin and a name that stay the same size on the screen** at any distance; from above the pin also points the way the player faces. The selected player is marked by a ring on the ground. A dead player's body **lies where they died**, dimmed, with a cross over it, and stays; a line joins victim and attacker at the moment of a kill. Each player is a handful of small meshes (32 players are a few hundred draw calls, fine); instancing is left for later if it ever matters.
*   **Smooth playback:** positions are interpolated between samples by their real times (`t`), so a 5-per-second track plays smoothly at any speed, and a gap (a skipped tick) is shown as a gap, not as a straight line.
*   **Who is selected by default.** The replay opens on **the viewer's own player** when the signed-in visitor took part in the round (their SteamID64 is in the round), with the follow camera and the level selector already on them. For a visitor who was not in the round the default is the victim of the round's first death, because that is where the round's story starts, and, with no deaths, the first player in the list. Clicking any player in the list selects them.
*   **Controls:** play and pause, speed (1x, 2x, 4x, 8x), a scrubber over the round's length with the deaths marked on it, a list of the players (click to select and follow), and the round's header (map, result, duration, roles). Phones are a target: touch scrubbing, a layout that works at 390 px.
*   **Implementation:** the viewer is a separate client-side bundle (three.js, its glTF loader and orbit controls, and our code for the timeline, interpolation and markers) loaded only on the replay page; the rest of the dashboard does not grow. The logic that is not drawing (decoding a track, interpolating by time, mapping a position to a level, choosing the background) is plain code that is unit-tested; the drawing is checked by rendering in a headless browser. The server renders no per-frame data. **A browser without WebGL** gets the floor-plan picture of the round's level and the list of deaths instead of the viewer.
*   **A replay shows where players were and when, not who could see whom.** The mesh is a simplified view of the map (RFC 11): floors and the upright faces of the world model only. It has no doors, glass, props, lifts or breakable walls, and no ceilings, so a player behind a crate looks exposed and one behind a window looks hidden. A replay that is used to judge "he could not have seen me" or "I should have seen him" would be judging by a picture that is not what the game used. So the viewer carries a fixed line saying so, no feature draws a sight line or a field of view on the mesh, and a report built on a replay is a reason to look at the round, never evidence of a line of sight. Stated here so that nobody later adds a view cone that looks authoritative.
*   **Pinned rounds:** an admin sees the pin button; everyone sees that a round is pinned.
*   **Spoilers:** a replay is only available after the round has ended, so it cannot help anyone in the running round (4.2).

*   **As built (step 3):** `GET /servers/<id>/rounds/<round_id>/replay` (page) and `.../track.json` (data), both for signed-in users (RFC 13); an anonymous visitor goes to sign-in and back (to the stats page when sign-in is closed). `track.json` is one document: the round, its players, the stored track, and the background with its levels, so the page needs one request besides the mesh. It is the same for every viewer, so one weak ETag serves all (built from the track's creation time and size, the pin, the bots choice and what is behind it); `If-None-Match` answers `304`; the body is gzipped by hand when the browser accepts it; it is logged as `VIEW_REPLAY` and limited to 30 a minute per account. The round list shows a replay link for rounds that have a track (to a signed-in visitor the page, otherwise a sign-in link, or "admins" when sign-in is closed) and a pin mark for everyone; the replay page has the pin button for admins, which brings them back to the page (the `next` field is checked like the sign-in one).
*   **The viewer** (`src/replay.ts`, built to `src/public/replay.js`, loaded only on the replay page; the logic that is not drawing is in `src/replaylogic.ts` with its own tests). Decisions the RFC left open: it **opens in the follow camera** on the default player (the viewer's own, else the first victim, else the first player); one orbit camera is shared by the 3D and follow modes and its pose is restored when coming back from another mode; the top view is orthographic with north up and pan and zoom only; **a position's level is the level whose floors reach its height, else the nearest, and where two reach it the one whose middle is nearer** (so stairs do not flip a player between levels at the boundary); with "follow the selected player's level" on (the default) the level selector follows them, and choosing a level by hand turns that off; players on a level above the one shown are hidden, those below are hidden by the floor and their name is dimmed; a pin and a name are the same size on the screen whatever the distance and the bodies are never enlarged (an orthographic camera does not do that for sprites by itself, so there they are sized by what the view shows); trails show the last four seconds; the kill line shows for two and a half seconds; a gap is a hidden marker, never a line; the plan background is a tile per floor cell (one instanced mesh per level), and with no map at all a grid fitted to the track. **A browser without WebGL** gets the list of players and deaths and a message, not a floor-plan picture (a smaller fallback than the RFC first proposed).
*   **Checked:** 19 tests of the logic, 24 of the routes, and by eye in headless Chromium on a real map's mesh (all four camera modes, the level cut, the phone width of 390 px), a floor plan, an empty grid and a browser with WebGL switched off. There is no automated test of the drawing. The synthetic plan in those checks was made from triangle centres, so it is sparse; a real plan from the bridge covers every cell.

### 3.6 Heat maps (not in this RFC, noted)
*Update (2026-11-04): the death heat map is built, as the killzones of [RFC 21](./RFC-021.md) (a picture on the map's page, from the stored death positions, filtered by weapon and level). Other heat maps (where traitors wait, where players walk) are still open.*

With tracks stored, a map's **death heat map** (every death on that map, drawn on its floor plan) is one query and one picture. It is out of scope here because aggregating across rounds raises its own questions (how many rounds, per role or team, how to avoid a handful of rounds dominating); a later RFC can cover it.

## 4. Security and Privacy

### 4.1 The recording must not hurt the game
Same rule as the stats, restated because positions are sampled while the round runs: a timer, not `Think`; `pcall` around the body; a kill switch (`traitordash_track 0` takes effect at the next tick); hard caps on players, ticks and bytes; nothing is sent during the round; a failed upload is dropped; and **a server with the ConVar off does nothing at all**, not even create the timer. On top of that, the sampling rate is **self-limiting** (3.2): each tick is timed against a budget, a busy server makes the interval longer or skips ticks, and a server that cannot afford the recording at all gets a `degraded` track, not a slower game. The acceptance test (section 7) compares the server's frame time with and without the recording.

### 4.2 What a track reveals, and to whom
*   A track says where every named player was, as often as five times a second, and (through team colours) who was a traitor. The round stats already publish names, SteamID64s, roles and kills on public pages, so a replay adds **behaviour** (where someone stood, who followed whom), not new kinds of identifying data. It is more revealing than a table of results all the same.
*   **A replay is not public.** The replay page, `track.json` and the map's mesh need a **signed-in user** (RFC 13: any Steam account, with admins as a subset). An anonymous visitor sees the round's result and a link that leads to sign-in. This is a deliberate trade: a replay cannot be linked from a forum or a Discord for someone without an account, but it can be limited per account, attributed, and kept away from unattended scraping. (If the operator has not turned open sign-in on, RFC 13's switch, only admins are signed in and replays are admin-only.)
*   **After the round only.** A track is uploaded when the round has ended, and there is no live view, so a replay cannot give anyone an advantage in the round being played.
*   **What an endpoint that serves megabytes costs.** A track can be several MB. So a track is **immutable**, and `track.json` is served with an `ETag` and `Cache-Control: private, max-age=3600` (private because the response is for a signed-in user; a removed track at worst stays in that browser's cache for an hour); it is sent **compressed** (HTTP compression, which shrinks this kind of JSON a lot); and it is **rate limited per account** (30 a minute, RFC 13 section 4.6) with `429` and `Retry-After`.
*   **Views are logged** (RFC 13 6.3): who opened which replay, once per user per round per hour, in the audit log, so an abusive pattern can be seen.
*   Pinning, the retention settings and deleting remain **admin-only** with a same-site `Origin` (RFC 7).
*   Tracks contain SteamID64s and nicknames, like the stats. Deleting a server deletes its tracks; the retention limits (3.4) bound how long unpinned tracks are kept. Removing a *player's* data on request is not in this RFC (the same is true of the stats today).

### 4.3 The upload endpoint
It is authenticated with the bridge's token and can only write a track for a round that the same server has already reported and that has none. Every number is range-checked and every length is bounded before anything is stored, so a hostile or buggy bridge cannot fill the database (the 8 MB body limit, the per-server track and byte limits) or write another server's data. A rejected track stores nothing.

## 5. Data Model
Migration v13:

```sql
CREATE TABLE round_tracks (
  server_id   TEXT NOT NULL REFERENCES servers(id) ON DELETE CASCADE,
  round_id    TEXT NOT NULL,
  map         TEXT NOT NULL,
  fingerprint TEXT,
  truncated   INTEGER NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  degraded    INTEGER NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  bytes       INTEGER NOT NULL,
  created_at  TEXT NOT NULL,
  pinned      INTEGER NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,       -- 1: never removed by the retention limits
  pinned_by   TEXT,                              -- the admin's SteamID64
  pinned_at   TEXT,
  data        TEXT NOT NULL,                 -- the validated track, JSON
  PRIMARY KEY (server_id, round_id),
  FOREIGN KEY (server_id, round_id) REFERENCES rounds(server_id, round_id) ON DELETE CASCADE
);

-- the per-server override of the retention limits (3.4); NULL means "use the instance default"
ALTER TABLE servers ADD COLUMN track_keep_count INTEGER;
ALTER TABLE servers ADD COLUMN track_keep_mb    INTEGER;
```
Positions are not exploded into rows (the times `t` live inside `data`): a round is read and drawn as a whole, and a row per sample would be hundreds of thousands of rows per day for no query that needs them. Aggregates (3.6) can be derived into their own table later.

## 6. Compatibility and Rollout
*   Additive and **off by default**: a bridge without the ConVar, or with it at `0`, behaves as today; an old bridge sends no tracks; an old backend answers `404` to `/api/v1/track` and the bridge logs it once and stops trying until the next map.
*   **Bridge and backend are independent.** The viewer says "no track for this round" for rounds without one.
*   Delivered in order: (1) the backend endpoint, storage and validation, with tests on synthetic tracks; (2) the bridge recording and upload, with the Fengari harness; (3) the viewer, built against stored synthetic tracks; (4) a real round on a real server to check cost and size.

## 7. Testing
*   **Backend:** a valid track is stored once and a repeat changes nothing; a track for an unknown round, another server's round, or a round that already has one is refused; mismatched array lengths, non-integers, out-of-range coordinates, overlapping segments, more than the caps, a body over 8 MB and a player who was not in the round are each a `400` and store nothing; the track is deleted with its round and its server; retention removes the oldest beyond the count and the byte limits, whichever is exceeded first, and keeps the newest; the built-in defaults, an instance default and a per-server value are applied in that order of precedence; a per-server value above the ceiling or below 0 is refused, an empty one falls back to the default; `0` accepts a track and stores nothing; a default above its ceiling or a non-number in the environment stops the start-up with a message; lowering a limit takes effect at the next upload and at start-up; a track larger than the size limit is a `413`; the usage shown is the real count and bytes; changing a limit needs an admin session with a same-site `Origin` and is audited; admin-only reads.
*   **Bridge (Fengari):** with the ConVar off there is no timer; with it on, each tick appends one sample per living player and none for the dead or for spectators; the interval grows when the (fake) clock says a tick cost more than the budget, shrinks again when it is cheap, never leaves its minimum and maximum, and a busy server (fake frame time above 1.5 and 3 times the tick interval) lengthens the interval and then skips ticks, recording a gap; a server that cannot meet the budget even at the slowest interval gets a `degraded` track; the interval is raised as the size cap approaches instead of cutting the round; the round-end line states ticks, interval range, average and worst cost, skips and size; the first value is absolute and the rest are differences that rebuild the exact positions; a death takes a final sample and records both positions; a revived player gets a second segment; the caps truncate and set the flag; an error inside the timer is caught and the round still ends and reports; the kill switch stops sampling at the next tick; the track is sent only after the round was accepted and is dropped on failure.
*   **Pinning and access:** a pinned track survives the count and size limits while unpinned ones go; pinning needs an admin session and a same-site `Origin` and is audited; the maximum number of pins is enforced; `track.json` and the replay answer `401` (data) or send an anonymous visitor to sign-in with `next` set (page), work for any signed-in user and for admins, hide bot rounds without `?bots=1`, carry an `ETag` and a private `max-age`, are rate limited per account with `429`, and `If-None-Match` answers `304`; a view is logged once per user, round and hour.
*   **Default selection:** a signed-in user who took part is selected, with the follow camera and level on them; a user who did not take part gets the victim of the first death; with no deaths, the first player; a SteamID64 that is not in the round selects nobody by mistake.
*   **Viewer:** the background is chosen in order (mesh, then plan tiles, then an empty grid) and the page says which; a mesh or plan with another fingerprint is flagged; the plan-as-tiles builder puts a tile at every floor cell at its height, grouped per level; markers are placed in the same coordinates as the map (a position that is on a known floor of the mesh lies on it); the level selector hides the levels above the chosen one; decoding rebuilds the positions from the shared tick times; interpolation uses the real times and leaves a gap a gap; a real round is rendered in a headless browser with each background (screenshots checked by eye, and a pixel check that something other than the background colour is drawn); the page without WebGL shows the fallback; the scrubber and speed move the right frame; a position maps to the right level and the right pixel for a known plan; no plan draws on a fitted grid; the page works at phone width.
*   **How the frame time is measured (built):** `traitordash_frametime round | start | stop | status | history` (`addon/lua/traitordash/frametime.lua`, console or superadmin only, nothing runs until started; its Think hook exists only while a measurement does, capped at 200 000 frames). It records the engine's `FrameTime()` and the wall time between Think calls, and prints one line per measurement: mean, 99th percentile and worst of each, how many frames took over 1.5 and 2 times the tick, the player count and whether tracking was on. **A dedicated server runs on a fixed tick, so this shows overruns (a hitch, a server that cannot keep up), not headroom**; headroom is `Svms` in the server's own `stats` command, to be read alongside. The tracker also prints how long encoding a track at round end took (the one hitch it can cause). The comparison is rounds with `traitordash_track 0` and `1` on the same map and with a similar number of players.
*   **A round for the real-server test, without players (built):** `traitordash_track_test [bots] [seconds] [off]` (`addon/lua/traitordash/track_test.lua`, server console only, refuses when a real player is online) restarts the round with 2-16 bots that **walk** (a random walk through the `StartCommand` hook: forward, a new heading every 1.5-4 s, a turn away after 0.6 s of standing against something, an occasional jump), has a traitor bot kill the others from 30% of the given time on so the round ends by itself, waits for the stats and the track to be sent, kicks the bots and puts `traitordash_track`, `traitordash_stats_bots` and `bot_zombie` back as it found them, also after a failure. With `off` tracking stays off: the control run for `traitordash_frametime`. The replay of such a round needs `?bots=1`. Bots walk straight into walls and stairs the way bots do, so the track shows a plausible but not a realistic round; it exercises the recording, the size and the replay, not player behaviour.
*   **Real server (the acceptance test for the sampling rate):** rounds with 10+ players with tracking off and on, compared on the server's frame time, the worst sampling tick cost, the interval range chosen by the controller, and the size per minute; the fastest allowed interval is only lowered below its default if the worst tick stays under the budget and the frame time does not move. Plus a visual check of one replay against what the admin remembers of the round.

### 7.2 Idea: the players' real model (2026-11-04)
The figure in the replay is a box-sized low-poly person. The backend can already read a model's `.mdl`, `.vvd` and `.vtx` from the game's own content (RFC 18, `GAME_CONTENT=1`), so the replay could draw each player's real playermodel instead. It would need the bridge to send each player's model path with the round (`ply:GetModel()`), the model in its default pose turned to face the player's yaw, tinted in the team colour (no textures), and the box figure for any model that cannot be found (Workshop playermodels are not in the game's content). Walking, crouching and a body lying down need skeleton animation, which is a much larger step. Now written up as [RFC 22](./RFC-022.md) (proposal; nothing built).

## 8. Alternatives Considered
*   **Server-side demos.** GMod records demos on the client, not on a dedicated server, so there is nothing to collect from the server, and a demo needs the game to play it back.
*   **Streaming positions while the round runs.** Allows a live view, but means a request every second per server, a failure mode during play, and public-spoiler problems. The single upload after the round avoids all three; a live view can be a later RFC.
*   **A separate 2D canvas viewer and a 3D viewer.** Two renderers to build and keep in step. One three.js scene with a top-down orthographic camera gives the 2D replay as a camera mode, and the plan-as-tiles background gives maps without a mesh the same viewer.
*   **A fixed interval.** Simple and predictable, but either too slow for a busy fight or too fast for a struggling server. A rate that adapts to a measured budget gets the most detail the server can afford.
*   **Sampling in `Think`.** Gives smoother tracks and puts the cost on every frame. A timer at 1 Hz is cheap enough, and replays do not need 66 samples a second.
*   **Only events (deaths and kills, with positions).** Almost free, but a death without the movement leading to it answers nothing about RDM or about where a traitor waited. It remains the fallback if the sampling proves too costly on real servers.
*   **A row per sample.** Easy to query, but orders of magnitude more rows for queries nothing needs yet (3.6).
*   **Tracking on by default.** Convenient, but positions of named players are a different kind of data from a result table, and the owner should choose it.

## 9. Open Questions
Gone through against the code and the real server on 2026-11-06; what is answered says how, what is open says why.

*   **Sampling parameters: answered, the default stays a choice.** Measured on the real server with 16 bots (section 13): at 0.10 s the average tick cost was 0.093 ms against the budget of 0.5 ms, nothing was skipped, the interval never had to grow, and no frame overran. A round of 289 s at 16 players is 415 KB encoded, so 0.1 s is affordable in size (the server keeps 200 tracks or 200 MB, and has 62 tracks). The default is still 0.2 s (section 12); changing it is the maintainer's choice, not a measurement. The tick costs of the real rounds with players are not known: the server's console is cleared when it restarts and those lines were gone.
*   **Compression: answered, not needed.** 415 KB for the longest round tried; no LZMA, no decoder.
*   **Public replays: decided against** (RFC 13): a replay needs a signed-in user. **Open:** whether a player can ask for their name to be hidden in replays.
*   **What else to record: everything (decided 2026-11-06), [RFC 26](./RFC-026.md).** The parts that move and what was done to them are recorded (RFC 20), the weapon of a death and the damage by weapon are in the statistics (RFC 21). Chat, shop purchases and bodies found are recorded since stage A of RFC 26; health and the rest follow in its later stages.
*   **Retention and pinning: answered as built.** 200 tracks and 200 MB per server by default, ceilings of 2,000 and 2,000 MB, at most 50 pinned (`TRACK_DEFAULTS` in `services/tracks.ts`); they are settings, so the numbers can change without code. The server holds 62 tracks, far under the default.
*   **Bots: answered.** Their rounds are recorded only with the bot switch (`traitordash_stats_bots`), hidden unless `?bots=1`, and an admin can delete every test round at once.
*   **Level of a position: smoothed (2026-11-06).** The cut that follows the selected player goes up at once but goes down only when the player has been found on the lower level for 0.4 s in a row (`holdLevel`), so stairs and a jump at a boundary no longer make it flicker or hide the player; a seek takes the level as it is. Before, nothing was held.  Earlier note: no smoothing was built. The level bar offers only the storeys a player can get to (section 12), which removes the plates under a map, but a player on stairs can still be on the neighbouring level for a moment. Nobody has reported it.
*   **Maps without a plan: probably moot.** The replay draws the map's own mesh (RFC 11, 18), which is stored for every map the server has played. What the replay does for a map without a mesh was not checked again.
*   **The mesh and Workshop terms: decided (2026-11-06).** Yes: the mesh derived from public Workshop content is shown to signed-in users (RFC 13), as built. The map file and the addon are deleted after parsing and only the derived mesh is kept (RFC 11, section 8). The terms themselves have not been read; this is the maintainer's decision.
*   **Mobile performance: partly answered.** The viewer was checked at a phone width of 390 px and has been used on a phone (RFC 22, 2026-11-04). No frame budget was set and there is no lighter mode.

### As built (deleting rounds and replays, added later)

An admin can delete a round's replay alone (`POST /servers/<id>/rounds/<round>/delete` with `what=replay`; the round stays in the statistics), a round with its replay (`what=round`: players, deaths, revivals and track), or every round only test bots played in (`POST /servers/<id>/rounds/delete-bots`). Buttons: the replay page (both), the position-tracks card on the stats page (test rounds, with their number), and a tick box per recent round on the stats page with "Delete selected rounds" (`POST /servers/<id>/rounds/delete-selected`, at most 100 at a time), for rounds that have no replay page or that real players played in. A pinned replay is deleted too, since asking is the decision. Admin session and same-site Origin are required and every deletion is audited (`TRACK_DELETED`, `ROUND_DELETED`, `BOT_ROUNDS_DELETED`). A round counts as a test round when it has at least one bot and no other player.

## 9. Follow camera and deaths: what a real round showed (2026-11-04)
A screen recording of the replay of a real round (a Lego round, on a phone) and a copy of the live database showed three things, all repeated on the copy:
*   **The follow camera went through walls.** It sits 300 units behind the player, so a player walking along the outer wall of the map put the camera outside it, and the screen filled with the back of the wall for seconds at a time (the camera was 140 units outside the map). The camera now looks along the way back from the player's head and stops 24 units before the first floor, wall or solid prop (`keepInside` in `replay.ts`; looked at every 50 ms, a few milliseconds on the largest map measured, 46 000 triangles and 911 props).
*   **The bodies hung in the air**, because the death's height was that of a spectator's eyes (RFC 22, 4b): fixed in the bridge and, for the tracks already stored, in the decoder.
*   Large grey shards that come and go at the top of the picture are the leaves of a palm tree close to the camera, not a fault of the map or the models. Not changed.

## 10. How the player moves (2026-11-04, RFC 22)
Each position sample of a track now also carries `s` (`track.lua`, `tracks.ts`): bit 1 crouching, bit 2 in the air, bit 4 in water, from `Crouching()`, `IsOnGround()` and `WaterLevel()`. It is optional on the way in (a game server of before sends none and its tracks are drawn as if standing) and costs about 2 bytes a sample. The replay picks the animation of the player's model from it and from the speed (RFC 22, 4c).

## 11. Why a deployment did not show, and what made the replay slow (2026-11-04)
*   **Caches.** The scripts under `/public/` are served for a week with `immutable`, at addresses without a version, and the replay's data (`track.json`) for an hour, with an ETag that did not change when the player model's address did. So a browser kept the replay script and the data of the build before and showed the still model for the moving one (found by asking `/health`, which showed the new build running, and the live script, which held the new code). Now every script of a page carries the code's version in its address (`?v=` and the source hash), the player model's address the same, `track.json` is revalidated each time (`no-cache`, a 304 when nothing changed), and its ETag includes the model's address and a format number.
*   **Speed.** Measured on a real round with the processor slowed four times (about a phone), per picture: the render 10 ms, the replay's own work 5 ms (the camera's look along its way back 2 ms, posing the models 1.3 ms). Changed: the props of the map are drawn as one mesh for each material (112 draw calls became 38; the separate ones stay, unseen, for the camera), the map's matrices are no longer worked out each frame, the models are posed 30 times a second, the camera looks every 120 ms, trails reuse their buffers, text is written to the page only when it differs, a touch screen draws at most one and a half pixels for each of the page's, and **while it plays the picture is made at most thirty times a second** (time goes on at the real rate). In the software renderer of the test machine a picture took 74 ms before and 41 ms after, without the models 63 ms.

## 12. The follow camera: smooth, and it sees through (2026-11-04)
*   **Smooth.** It stepped five times a second because it followed the last *sample* of the player, which changes that often (a sample every 180 ms on the real server, so a step of 35 to 55 units at running speed, a fifth of the camera's distance), and turned with the sampled view angle. It now follows the player's place between the samples and settles into it (`damp`, `dampAngle`: a fraction of the way that depends on the time, not on the frame rate; 70 ms for the place, 200 ms for the turn), and goes straight there when time jumps or the selection changes. Measured on a real round while it played: the place it tracks moved at 308 units a second at the median and 483 at the 99th percentile (a runner's), with none of the spikes of a stepped camera.
*   **It looks through what is between it and the player**, instead of being pulled forward (the pull-in and its ray are gone). Everything on a cone from the camera to the player's head is faded with a screen-door pattern, nothing beyond the player: a **prop as a whole** (each vertex carries the middle and size of its prop, so a tree or a car fades as one), a **wall** by the pixel, a **floor** only where it is above the line of sight. The cone is 70 units wide at the player and 400 at the camera. It is a few lines in the shaders of the map's materials (`seeThrough`), so it costs nothing per object and works on the merged props. With it the camera outside the map's outer wall, which used to see the back of the wall, sees the players.
*   **Frame rate.** A picture is made for every frame the screen asks for (60 on a device that can) unless a picture costs the page more than 9 ms (a moving average that ignores a first picture's shader compiling), when it falls back to thirty a second until a picture costs under 6 ms again; the skeletons are posed with every picture or every 33 ms in that case.
*   **A large prop is faded by the pixel (2026-11-05).** On a real round (Lego) the follow camera sometimes sat behind or inside a building and showed one grey surface with the player nowhere in sight: a prop is faded as a whole by where its middle is, and the middle of a building can be far from the line of sight while a corner of it is in the way (or the camera is inside it), so it did not fade at all. Found by hiding the kinds of surface one at a time on the real round (only hiding the props showed the player). A prop with a radius over 200 units (`BIG_PROP`) is now faded by the pixel, as a wall is; the smaller ones (trees, cars, crates) are still faded as one. Checked on the six moments of the recording where it failed.
*   **An object whose middle is behind the camera (2026-11-05).** The flicker the maintainer saw in the first three seconds of a Lego round was the camera passing through a helicopter: its middle was a little behind the camera, which the fade left out ("behind the camera is not in the way"), so for two frames the helicopter went from half faded to solid, filled the screen and hid the player. The limit now allows for the object's size (`t < -0.05 - r / length`), so an object that surrounds the camera fades; a pixel keeps the plain limit. Found by capturing the frames every 50 ms around the time and comparing each with the one before (two frames differed by 35 where the others differ by 3 to 7); after the change the largest difference is 7.7.
*   **A ledge that hides the feet was never faded (2026-11-05, the maintainer: "see-through is not perfect yet for multi-level").** On 67th Way a player in a sunken passage was seen with their legs and the ring at their feet cut off by the grass of the level above: the lip of the ground between the camera and the passage. A floor is faded only where it is above the line from the camera to the player's feet, with 10 units to spare, but the floor that hides the feet is *on* that line (the pixel that hides a point lies on the ray to it), so it was never above it. The test is now "not more than 24 units below the line" together with "more than 16 units above the height of the feet" (the floor they stand on stays). Reproduced on the round of 2026-11-03 at 0:57 before the change and checked after on three moments. What is still not seen through: the far side of a faded wall is the empty background where the map has no faces behind it.
*   **More of the surroundings is seen (2026-11-05, the maintainer: "the cutout needs to go higher", "I'd like to see more of Tonda").** The cone around the line of sight is wider at the player (160 units, 400 at the camera; it was 70 to 400) and reaches higher than it is wide: what lies above the line is measured at 0.55 of its height, so it fades from 1.8 times as far. A floor or ledge above the player's feet fades for another 240 units past the player (walls and props still stop 8 units short), so a ledge in front of someone standing behind the player no longer hides them. Checked on the 67th Way round at 0:58 (the passage, the crates and the second player in it) and two other moments. Not done: lines of sight to other players (the maintainer asked for a wider fade around the followed player instead).

*   **The list of rounds was cut at 15 (found 2026-11-05).** The Rounds tab of the statistics showed the latest 15 rounds and had no way to the older ones, though 62 replays were kept: the maintainer asked where the older replays were. A replay could only be reached by its address, and an admin could only delete the rounds shown. The list is now paged: 25 rounds a page, the newest first, "Newer" and "Older" as plain links (`?page=n`, which keep the embed and the options and work in the game's window), and a footer that says "Rounds 26 to 50 of 66". A page past the end is the last page, a bad number the first; the overview keeps its short list. Tests in `rounds_paging.test.tsx` (RFC 17, the same page).
*   **The level bar offers the storeys a player can get to (2026-11-05).** The mesh has a storey for every height with a floor, also a plate under the map that no one can reach (Minecraft B7 Summer: level 2); the bar offered it. It now offers the storeys that can be reached from the spawn points and the places deaths were recorded; see RFC 21, 12.
*   **A smooth curve through the samples (2026-11-05).** Positions and the view direction were interpolated in straight lines, so the speed and the direction of a player changed abruptly at every sample, five times a second at the default rate; the replay looked like it stuttered (the maintainer's "5/s stutter"). `poseAt` now draws a monotone cubic (PCHIP: the harmonic mean of the slopes either side of a sample, zero at a turning point, so it never goes beyond the samples on either side and cannot cut a corner through a wall further than the player's own steps did) through the samples at their real times, per axis; the view direction is unwrapped to one continuous angle first, so a turn across 0 is not a spin. It passes exactly through every sample, is a straight line for a steady walk, and a gap stays a gap. It costs the game server nothing and improves every track already recorded. **Measured** on a real round (44,163 frames of all its players, 60 a second, the jumps of respawns left out): the change of velocity from frame to frame, in the worst 1% of frames, is 3,610 units/s² instead of 9,705 (63% less), at the 95th percentile 2,267 instead of 3,618, and the largest distance between the curve and the straight line over the same samples is 12.9 units. The distance a player has gone (which times the legs' steps) still follows the straight lines between samples. Tests in `replaylogic.test.ts`.
*   **The fastest sampling is 0.05 s (2026-11-05).** The floor was 0.1 s, a guess of this RFC's first version that was never measured; nothing technical set it (the controller already slows the sampling when a tick costs more than the budget or the server's frames get long). The maintainer asked why not sample faster, and the smooth curve above and a faster rate are two separate gains: the curve improves every track already recorded for free, a faster rate adds real information (a quick turn, a strafe, a jump) at a cost to the game server and to the size (about 42 KB a round at 0.2 s, so 170 KB at 0.05 s). The floor is now 0.05 s in the bridge (`track.lua`, `settings.lua`) and in the dashboard's list (`bridgeSettings.ts`, RFC 23); the default stays 0.2 s until the cost has been measured on a play night (`traitordash_frametime round`), and a round is cut at 40 000 ticks by default (33 minutes at 0.05 s), a limit that is now the setting `traitordash_track_max_ticks` (RFC 23, 1,000 to 100,000; the backend's limit is the ceiling). Tests follow the new range.
*   **Leaves are drawn with their own textures (2026-11-05).** The Yucatan replay was a pile of grey shards around the player (the maintainer: "WTF is this replay?!"). The map is a jungle: 356 of its 504 placed props are plants, and the game draws their leaves with textures whose alpha cuts the leaf out; this view read no textures, so each plant was 270 to 360 opaque grey triangles. A first answer, a switch that hides the leaves (off by default), was called what it was, a way of not looking, and is not the fix. The fix is RFC 18, 4.6: the textures are read from the map's own pack and the leaves are drawn cut out, so the jungle is trees, bushes and grass. The "Leaves" switch beside "Walls" stays (on by default), to see the ground through the plants, and is hidden for a map without any. The pages' policy (`connect-src 'self'`) stops the 3D loader from reading the textures inside the file, which was found by looking at why the first build showed white shards: the loader reads them as images instead (`mapLoader`, `sceneKit.ts`), and the policy is as strict as it was. Checked on the same round and moment before and after.
*   **The follow camera did not see through (2026-11-05, the maintainer: "follow-camera cutout/see-through not working!").** The round of Yucatan at 0:00: Tonda stood behind a wall, a ledge and a tree crown, all solid. Found by taking the map apart in the browser (the levels one at a time, the leaf meshes one at a time) and working the fade out for the real positions, four causes, each fixed: (1) **a wall 17 units in front of the player stayed solid**: nothing is faded in the last stretch before the head, which was 8% of the line (about 25 units) and is now a fixed 8 units; (2) **a ledge below the line to the head still hid the legs**: a floor was faded only above the line to the head (a ceiling), and is now faded above the line to the feet, so a ledge that hides part of the body goes, while the floor under the feet (below that line) stays; (3) **a tree crown whose middle was by the player stayed solid**: a prop was faded as a whole by where its middle is, and the end of the line applied to the middle, so a crown hanging over the player's head was skipped; props over 60 units in radius (it was 200) are faded by the pixel, so a crown is cut out where it is in the way and keeps its shape elsewhere; (4) **a material with a single mesh was never faded**: the fade needs an attribute that only merged meshes got, and the merge skipped a material with one mesh, which with a texture for each species of plant is common; a single mesh is merged too. Checked on the same round and moment before and after, and at 9 s and 31 s. It was not found by the earlier tests because they were taken on maps with no jungle: the shader has no unit test (it needs a browser), only these before-and-after pictures.

*   **What this does not show:** the rounds with real players (62 tracks are stored): their tick costs are not here, since the server's console is cleared when it restarts; 16 players is more than the server has (at most about 10); the bots walk and do not open doors (0 inputs, which RFC 20 stage 2 needs a play night for); and the round was on one map.

## 14. The cutaway and the follow camera (2026-11-06)
The level bar hides the levels above the chosen one and cuts the map away a little above its floors, so a building can be looked into from outside. "Follow the selected player's level" (on by default) used that in every camera. In the follow camera, which stands inside the map, it took the tops off the walls, left props floating in a black void, and on some maps hid half the terrain (a round on `ttt_cape_town_revamped` that the maintainer found, then 13 more moments on `ttt_yucatan`, `ttt_lttp_kakariko_a5_fixed`, `ttt_airbus_b3`, `ttt_pwp_v4`, `ttt_forest_final`, `ttt_67thway_rework_b1` and others, rendered locally in a headless browser from the stored rounds, before and after).

*   **Cause.** A level is a slice of the whole map, walls and ground included, so hiding the levels above the player takes the upper part of everything on them away. The level ranges also overlap a lot: on `ttt_cape_town_revamped`, `ttt_airbus_b3`, `ttt_lttp_kakariko_a5_fixed` and `ttt_yucatan` a player is inside the range of two or more levels in 68 to 94% of all samples, so which level they are "on" is not well defined, and the cut was often the wrong height. The cut plane was not the main fault: a build with no plane showed the same black band. (A first change on the same day, no plane for the follow camera, was therefore not enough.)
*   **Now.** While the follow camera follows the player's level, nothing is hidden (`shownLevels` in `replaylogic.ts`); what is between the camera and the player is faded out by the line-of-sight fade that was already there (a roof over the player turns to a dither). A level picked on the level bar is kept in every camera, and the orbit and top cameras still cut away the levels above, as before.
*   **Not changed.** Some maps still show black where the map has no mesh (a shaft in `ttt_67thway_rework_b1`, the dark area in `ttt_pwp_v4`); that was there before and is a matter of the map's mesh, not of the cut.
*   **The level the cut follows** still goes up at once and down after 0.4 s (`holdLevel`); that matters for the orbit and top cameras now. On the four maps above most level changes are real moves between levels, not flicker (282 of 6 rounds on Cape Town, 273 left with the hold), so the hold is a small help.
*   **The see-through fade (2026-11-06).** What lies between the follow camera and the player is faded out, so the player is not hidden (section 12). It faded too much: the cone around the line from the camera to the head was 160 to 400 units wide and a floor or ledge above the player's feet was faded up to 240 units *beyond* the player (made for a second player standing behind a ledge in the same passage). On `ttt_cape_town_revamped` a plank in front of the player's shins became a fan of dither with the ice showing through it, and on `ttt_yucatan` the level in front went see-through for no visible reason (the maintainer's reports). Now the cone is 80 to 180 units and nothing is faded beyond the player (`FADE` in `replaylogic.ts`; narrower, 50 and 50, left stippled edges on big props). Looked at on 14 rendered moments of 11 maps, and on the seconds around the Cape Town one: the planks stay solid, the player stays visible indoors (`ttt_airbus_b3`, `ttt_lego`). Cost: a big slab such as the roof over the player on `ttt_airbus_b3` stays solid where it used to be dithered, and a second player hidden behind a ledge beyond the selected one is no longer shown through it.
